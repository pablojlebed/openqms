## 1. Purpose

To define how the laboratory receives, identifies, handles, protects, stores, retains, and disposes of items submitted for testing, so that the integrity of each item is maintained from the moment it enters the laboratory's custody until its final disposition, and so that the interests of both the customer and the laboratory are protected.

## 2. Scope

This procedure applies to all items received by the laboratory for testing, regardless of how they arrive (courier, hand delivery, internal transfer from sampling personnel) or who submits them (external customer, internal department). It covers:

- **Receipt and logging** — Recording arrival, checking items against the accepted contract, and assigning a unique identity
- **Examination at receipt** — Inspecting condition, quantity, and packaging, and recording any anomalies or deviations from what was expected
- **Identification and traceability** — Maintaining an unambiguous link between the item, its sub-divisions, and its test results throughout its life in the laboratory
- **Handling and protection** — Preventing deterioration, contamination, loss, or damage during transport within the laboratory, preparation, storage, and testing
- **Storage** — Maintaining items under appropriate conditions while awaiting or between tests
- **Retention, return, and disposal** — Managing items after testing is complete, including hold periods, return to the customer, and safe disposal

For the battery materials laboratory, items received for testing typically include:

- Cathode powders (NMC, LFP, LCO) in drums, bags, or small vials
- Anode materials (graphite, silicon-composite powders)
- Electrolyte solutions and solvents — flammable, air- and moisture-sensitive
- Electrode slurries and binder solutions
- Separator films
- Current collector foils (aluminum, copper)
- Assembled electrodes or small cells submitted for physical or chemical characterization (e.g., cross-sectioning, elemental mapping) rather than electrical cycling

This procedure does not cover the laboratory's own sampling activities, which are addressed by [Procedure 12 — Sampling](../12-sampling/index.md), or the specification of storage environmental conditions, which is addressed by [Procedure 07 — Facilities and Environmental Conditions](../07-facilities-environment/index.md). It applies from the point an item arrives at the laboratory, whether that item was collected by the laboratory's own sampling personnel or submitted directly by the customer.

:::note[Relationship to sampling]
When the laboratory performs its own sampling, the transition from "sample collected in the field" to "item received at the laboratory" is the handoff point between [Procedure 12](../12-sampling/index.md) and this procedure. The sample is logged as a received item at the moment it enters laboratory custody, and this procedure governs everything that happens to it from that point forward.
:::

## 4. Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Item** | The material, sample, or object submitted to the laboratory for testing. Used interchangeably with "test item" in this procedure; a calibration item is handled under the same principles where applicable. |
| **Receipt** | The point at which an item passes into the laboratory's custody and responsibility. |
| **Unique identification** | An identifier assigned to an item at receipt that remains associated with the item, and with every portion or derivative of it, for as long as it remains in the laboratory's custody. |
| **Chain of custody** | A documented, unbroken record of who has had possession or control of an item from receipt to final disposition. |
| **Anomaly (at receipt)** | Any condition of an item, on arrival, that differs from what was expected or agreed — including damage, insufficient quantity, incorrect labeling, compromised packaging, or signs of degradation. |
| **Quarantine** | The controlled, segregated holding of an item whose suitability for testing has not yet been confirmed, pending a decision by the Technical Manager or agreement with the customer. |
| **Nonconforming item** | An item that, after examination, is found unsuitable for testing as submitted — due to damage, insufficient quantity, incorrect identity, or failure to meet a stated requirement. |
| **Retention period** | The length of time an item (or its remains) is kept by the laboratory after testing is complete, before it is returned to the customer or disposed of. |
| **Disposition** | The final action taken with respect to an item after testing and any applicable retention period: return to the customer, further retention, or disposal. |
| **Sub-division** | Any division of a received item into smaller portions for the purpose of testing (e.g., taking a test portion from a received sample), or transfer of an item or portion of it between personnel, locations, or organizations. |

## 6. Procedure — Receipt of Items

### 6.1 Logging receipt

When an item arrives at the laboratory, **Reception personnel**:

1. Confirm the delivery against the accepted contract or request (see [Procedure 09 — Customer Service](../09-customer-service/index.md)) — customer name, job reference, expected number and description of items.
2. Record the date and time of receipt, the method of delivery (courier, hand delivery, internal transfer), and who received it.
3. Enter the item into the laboratory's sample register, generating a unique identification code (Section 7).

If an item arrives that was not expected, or that cannot be matched to an accepted contract, it is placed in quarantine and the **Quality Manager** or **Technical Manager** is notified before it proceeds further.

### 6.2 Examination at receipt

Before an item is accepted into the normal workflow, **Reception personnel** examine it and record:

- Quantity received, compared against what was requested or expected
- Packaging condition (intact, sealed, damaged, leaking)
- Container labeling and whether it matches the accompanying paperwork
- Visible condition of the item itself, to the extent it can be assessed without opening sealed packaging (e.g., color, physical state, signs of contamination or degradation)
- Any temperature indicators, humidity indicators, or other condition indicators included with the shipment
- For items with a stated hazard classification (e.g., flammable solvents, corrosive electrolyte components), confirmation that safety documentation (safety data sheet, transport documentation) has been provided

This examination is recorded in the **Item Receipt and Condition Record** ([Appendix 13-App-01](./appendix-01-item-receipt-condition-record)).

Examples of routine checks for the battery materials laboratory:

| Item type | What is checked at receipt |
|---|---|
| **Cathode/anode powder (drum or bag)** | Seal integrity, container material intact, no moisture ingress, label matches request, weight approximately as expected |
| **Electrolyte solution/solvent** | Container sealed, no leakage, appropriate secondary containment present, safety data sheet provided, volume approximately as expected |
| **Slurry samples** | Container sealed, no visible drying or separation, no leakage |
| **Separator film** | Packaging intact, no visible tears, folds, or contamination |
| **Metal foils** | Packaging intact, no visible oxidation, scratching, or creasing |
| **Assembled electrodes/small cells (for characterization)** | Packaging intact, no visible physical damage, swelling, or leakage; confirmation that the item is not electrically live in a way that presents a handling hazard |

### 6.3 Recording anomalies

If the examination identifies an anomaly — damage, insufficient quantity, a labeling discrepancy, a broken seal, signs of degradation, or anything else inconsistent with what was expected — it is recorded in full on the Item Receipt and Condition Record, including:

- A description of the anomaly
- Photographic evidence where practical (e.g., a damaged container, a compromised seal)
- The name of the person who identified it and the date

### 6.4 Deciding whether to proceed

When an anomaly is identified, the item is placed in quarantine and the **Technical Manager** decides how to proceed:

1. **Proceed without reservation** — If the anomaly does not affect the item's suitability for the requested testing (e.g., minor cosmetic damage to outer packaging with the inner container intact), testing may proceed. The decision and its basis are documented.
2. **Proceed with a documented reservation** — If the anomaly may affect the result but does not make testing impossible (e.g., a cathode powder sample that shows signs of moisture exposure, where moisture content is not the parameter being measured), the laboratory may proceed but must record the deviation and reflect it in the test report, so that anyone relying on the results understands the condition the item was received in.
3. **Contact the customer before proceeding** — If there is genuine doubt about whether the item is suitable for testing (e.g., insufficient quantity to perform all requested tests, or a sample that appears to be a different material than described), the laboratory contacts the customer for instructions before proceeding.
4. **Decline the item** — If the item cannot reasonably be tested as received (e.g., severely degraded, incorrectly identified, or presenting a safety hazard the laboratory is not equipped to manage), the laboratory declines to proceed, informs the customer, and documents the reason.

The decision, its rationale, and any customer communication are recorded on the Item Receipt and Condition Record.

### 6.5 Rejecting hazardous or non-compliant items

An item is rejected outright, without opening or further handling, if:

- It presents a hazard the laboratory is not equipped or authorized to manage (e.g., a fully assembled cell submitted for testing that requires electrical safety controls or transport certification the laboratory does not have)
- It lacks required safety documentation for a known hazardous material (e.g., an electrolyte solvent with no safety data sheet and unknown composition)
- Its packaging has failed in a way that presents an immediate risk (e.g., a leaking flammable solvent)

Rejected items are handled per the laboratory's safety procedures, and the customer is informed of the rejection and the reason without delay.

## 7. Procedure — Identification and Traceability

### 7.1 Assigning unique identification

Every item receives a unique identification code at the point of receipt, generated by the laboratory's sample register. This code:

- Links the item to the customer, the job or request reference, and the Item Receipt and Condition Record
- Remains associated with the item for as long as it is in the laboratory's custody
- Is carried forward to every sub-division of the item — test portions, digestion solutions, prepared extracts, or any other derivative created during testing

### 7.2 Labeling

Labels applied to items and their sub-divisions must be durable enough to survive the conditions the item will encounter in the laboratory, including:

- Solvent resistance for items handled with electrolyte solvents or used in wet chemistry preparation
- Temperature resistance for items placed in refrigerated or frozen storage
- Legibility after handling with gloves, in fume hoods, or in humidity-controlled areas

Labels are applied before an item is moved from the receiving area, and before any sub-division is created.

### 7.3 Traceability through sub-division and transfer

As an item moves through the laboratory — from storage to preparation, from preparation to an instrument, or between analysts — its identity is preserved:

- Each test portion, aliquot, or prepared extract is labeled with a reference back to the parent item's unique identification code
- Transfers between personnel (e.g., handoff from Reception to an analyst, or between analysts for sequential preparation steps) are recorded where the laboratory's procedures call for chain-of-custody tracking
- If any portion of an item is sent to a subcontractor, the transfer is recorded per [Procedure 06 — Externally Provided Products and Services](../06-externally-provided-services/index.md), and the subcontractor's identification of the item is cross-referenced to the laboratory's own identification code

### 7.4 Verifying identification

Before reporting a result, the analyst confirms that the identification on the test portion or prepared sample matches the identification recorded for the item throughout its preparation history. Any discrepancy is investigated before the result is reported, and treated as a potential nonconformity per [Procedure 14](../14-complaints-nonconformity/index.md) if it cannot be resolved.

## 8. Procedure — Handling and Protection of Items

### 8.1 General principles

Personnel handling items observe the following general principles:

- Minimize the time an item spends outside its required storage or handling conditions (e.g., minimize exposure of moisture-sensitive powders to ambient humidity)
- Use tools and containers appropriate to the material, and clean or dedicated to avoid cross-contamination
- Wear personal protective equipment appropriate to the material's hazards, as identified on the safety data sheet
- Handle items only to the extent necessary for the work being performed — avoid unnecessary opening, transfer, or exposure
- Report any damage, spillage, or degradation observed during handling immediately, rather than proceeding and noting it later

### 8.2 Handling requirements by material type

| Item type | Key handling requirements |
|---|---|
| **Cathode/anode powders (moisture-sensitive)** | Handle in low-humidity conditions where required; reseal containers promptly after use; avoid prolonged exposure to ambient air for hygroscopic materials |
| **Electrolyte solutions/solvents (flammable, air- or moisture-sensitive)** | Handle in a fume hood; keep away from ignition sources; use grounded, appropriately rated containers; minimize headspace and exposure time to limit moisture uptake or solvent loss |
| **Slurries** | Keep sealed when not in active use to prevent drying or phase separation; re-homogenize before sub-sampling if settling has occurred, and record that this was done |
| **Separator films** | Handle with clean gloves to avoid contamination from skin oils; avoid folding, creasing, or tearing; store flat where practical |
| **Metal foils** | Handle with gloves to prevent oxidation and fingerprint contamination; avoid scratching the surface, which can affect subsequent analysis |
| **Assembled electrodes/small cells (characterization only)** | Handle to avoid mechanical damage or short-circuiting of any residual charge; store away from other items in case of unexpected venting or leakage; follow any specific precautions identified at receipt |

### 8.3 Preventing cross-contamination

Because several materials handled by the laboratory share elements that are measured at trace levels in other work (see [Procedure 07 — Facilities and Environmental Conditions](../07-facilities-environment/index.md)), handling of one item must not compromise another:

- Tools used for powder handling are cleaned between items, or dedicated to specific material families where cross-contamination risk is high
- Items are not stored or handled in physical proximity to activities that could contaminate them (e.g., open powder handling near a sample awaiting moisture analysis)
- Personal protective equipment is changed or cleaned between handling of different customers' items where contamination could affect confidentiality or result integrity

### 8.4 Protecting integrity throughout the workflow

The requirements of this section apply at every stage an item passes through the laboratory — not only at receipt and in storage, but during preparation, while awaiting analysis on an instrument queue, during the test itself, and while awaiting disposition afterward. An item that is handled correctly at receipt but left exposed to unsuitable conditions on a bench overnight has still had its integrity compromised.

## 9. Procedure — Storage of Items

### 9.1 Storage conditions

Items are stored under conditions appropriate to their nature, as defined by the **Technical Manager** and monitored per [Procedure 07 — Facilities and Environmental Conditions](../07-facilities-environment/index.md). Typical storage categories for the battery materials laboratory:

| Storage category | Typical items | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Ambient, general storage** | Non-sensitive solid materials, metal foils | Room temperature, general laboratory environment |
| **Desiccated / low-humidity storage** | Moisture-sensitive cathode and anode powders | Controlled low humidity, sealed containers with desiccant where required |
| **Flammable materials cabinet** | Electrolyte solvents and solutions | Dedicated flammable-rated storage cabinet, away from ignition sources and oxidizers |
| **Refrigerated storage** | Slurries or solutions requiring cold storage to prevent degradation | 2–8 °C, continuously monitored |
| **Quarantine area** | Items awaiting a disposition decision after an anomaly at receipt | Physically or clearly logically separated from items cleared for testing |

### 9.2 Segregation

Items are segregated to prevent incompatible materials from being stored together, and to protect confidentiality and integrity:

- Flammable solvents are segregated from oxidizing materials, per standard chemical storage compatibility practice
- Items awaiting testing are segregated from items that have completed testing and are awaiting disposition, to avoid confusion
- Where required by the customer, items are stored in a manner that protects confidentiality from other customers' materials

### 9.3 Tracking storage location

The location of every item is recorded and kept current in the **Item Storage and Disposal Register** ([Appendix 13-App-02](./appendix-02-item-storage-disposal-register)), so that any item can be located without delay. When an item is moved between storage locations, the register is updated.

### 9.4 Access control

Storage areas — particularly flammable material cabinets, refrigerated storage, and quarantine areas — are accessible only to authorized personnel, consistent with the access controls defined in [Procedure 07](../07-facilities-environment/index.md).

## 10. Procedure — Retention, Return, and Disposal

### 10.1 Retention period

After testing is complete, items (or the remaining portion of an item) are retained for a defined period before final disposition. The retention period is determined by:

- Customer requirements or contractual agreement
- Regulatory requirements applicable to the material or the test
- The laboratory's own policy for supporting potential retests, complaints, or disputes

Unless a longer period is agreed, the default retention period is defined by the **Technical Manager** and documented in the applicable method or SOP.

### 10.2 Deciding disposition

At the end of the retention period, the **Technical Manager** or **Quality Manager** determines the disposition of each item:

- **Return to customer** — Where the customer has requested return, or where the item retains commercial value and no agreement specifies laboratory disposal
- **Extended retention** — Where there is a specific reason to hold the item longer (e.g., an open complaint, a pending retest, a regulatory hold)
- **Disposal** — Where the item is not to be returned and no reason exists to retain it further

The disposition decision and its date are recorded in the Item Storage and Disposal Register.

### 10.3 Returning items to customers

When an item is returned:

- It is packaged appropriately for transport, including any hazard labeling and documentation required for materials such as flammable electrolyte solvents
- Any applicable transport regulations for the material are followed (e.g., dangerous goods documentation)
- The transfer is recorded, including date, method of transport, and confirmation of receipt by the customer where practical

### 10.4 Disposal of items

Items designated for disposal are disposed of in a manner appropriate to the material:

| Material type | Disposal route |
|---|---|
| **Non-hazardous solid materials** | General waste, per local waste management requirements |
| **Electrolyte solvents and hazardous chemical residues** | Licensed hazardous waste contractor, per applicable chemical waste regulations |
| **Battery-related materials (electrode materials, assembled cells)** | Licensed battery/electronic waste recycling route, per applicable regulations |
| **Sharps or contaminated consumables used in item preparation** | Per the laboratory's standard sharps/chemical waste procedures |

Disposal of hazardous items requires sign-off from the **Safety Officer**. Every disposal is recorded in the Item Storage and Disposal Register, including the date, method, quantity, and who authorized it.

### 10.5 Retained item register

The Item Storage and Disposal Register serves as the single point of reference for the current status and location of every item, from receipt through final disposition, and is reviewed periodically by the **Quality Manager** to identify items approaching the end of their retention period.

## Why this section exists

Section 13 addresses custody: from the moment an item crosses the laboratory's threshold to the moment it leaves again, whether as a returned sample or as waste, the laboratory is responsible for what happens to it. Clause 7.4 exists because a perfectly executed test on a compromised item produces a number that is precise, defensible, and wrong for the question the customer actually asked.

### Receipt is a decision point, not a formality

It is tempting to treat receipt as administrative — log it, shelve it, move on. But receipt is the laboratory's only opportunity to catch a problem before it becomes embedded in a result. A leaking electrolyte container, a cathode powder drum with a broken seal, a sample quantity too small to complete the requested tests — all of these are far easier to address at the door than after three days of preparation and analysis have already been invested.

This is why Section 6 treats examination at receipt as a distinct, documented step with defined outcomes: proceed, proceed with a caveat, consult the customer, or decline. Each of these is a legitimate outcome. The failure mode is not choosing "proceed with a caveat" instead of "consult the customer" — reasonable people can disagree on where that line sits. The failure mode is skipping the examination altogether and discovering the problem only when a result looks strange.

### Identification is the thread that holds the result together

A test result is only meaningful if it can be connected, without ambiguity, back to the specific item it was produced from. This sounds obvious until you consider how many transformations an item goes through: a drum becomes a composite sample, becomes a sub-sample, becomes a digestion solution, becomes a diluted aliquot in an ICP-OES autosampler tray. If the identity is lost or confused at any step in that chain, the final number is disconnected from its origin — and no amount of analytical rigor downstream can repair that.

This is why Section 7 insists that unique identification survives every sub-division and transfer, not just the initial receipt. The label on the digestion vessel matters as much as the label on the drum.

### Handling requirements are material-specific, not generic

A laboratory that writes "handle samples carefully" into its procedures has not actually specified anything. What "careful" means for a moisture-sensitive cathode powder (minimize air exposure, reseal quickly) is different from what it means for a flammable electrolyte solvent (fume hood, ignition source control) or a delicate separator film (avoid creasing, avoid skin contact). Section 8 deliberately breaks handling requirements down by material type, because generic guidance produces generic — and therefore ineffective — behavior.

### Storage is where forgotten items accumulate risk

Every laboratory eventually discovers a shelf of samples nobody has looked at in months — retained past any useful purpose, taking up freezer space, or in the case of flammable materials, representing an unmanaged hazard. This happens because retention and disposition are treated as an afterthought rather than a defined step with an owner and a trigger.

Section 10 exists to prevent this drift. A retention period that is defined, tracked, and reviewed converts an indefinitely growing pile of "stuff we should probably deal with eventually" into a managed inventory with a clear endpoint for every item. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake — for hazardous materials, an unmanaged retention pile is a genuine safety liability, not just a tidiness problem.

### Rejecting an item is a legitimate outcome, not a failure

Laboratories sometimes feel pressure to accept and test whatever arrives, on the theory that turning work away is bad for the customer relationship. Section 6.5 pushes back on this. An item that presents a hazard the laboratory cannot safely manage, or that lacks the information needed to handle it safely, should be rejected — clearly, promptly, and with an explanation. A laboratory that tests an item it should have declined is not being accommodating. It is accepting a risk on the customer's behalf that the customer never asked it to accept.

## Common nonconformities

Clause 7.4 is assessed by tracing specific items — auditors will often pick a job at random and ask to see the full chain: what was recorded at receipt, how the item was identified, where it was stored, and what ultimately happened to it. Gaps anywhere in that chain are easy to find and are treated seriously because they undermine confidence in every result the item produced. The examples below reflect common findings.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-major">Major</span> No record of item condition at receipt

*"The laboratory received 12 drums of cathode powder from a customer. The sample register recorded the date of receipt and a job number, but no examination of container condition, seal integrity, or quantity was documented. When asked whether the drums had been inspected on arrival, the receiving technician said 'they looked fine' but could not point to any record."*

An undocumented visual impression is not evidence. If a question later arises about whether the material was compromised before it reached the laboratory, there is nothing to refer to. Section 6.2 requires the examination at receipt to be recorded at the time it happens, not reconstructed from memory afterward.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-major">Major</span> Anomaly identified but not escalated or documented

*"A container of electrolyte solvent arrived with a damaged cap and visible evidence of leakage inside the outer packaging. The receiving technician transferred the remaining solvent to a new container and proceeded with the requested testing. No record of the damage was made, the Technical Manager was not consulted, and the customer was not informed."*

Solvent loss through a damaged container can change the concentration of the remaining material — precisely the kind of change that would affect a test result. By quietly working around the problem instead of documenting and escalating it, the technician removed the customer's ability to understand the condition their sample arrived in, and removed the laboratory's ability to demonstrate that the result is trustworthy. Section 6.3 and 6.4 exist specifically to prevent this kind of silent accommodation.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-major">Major</span> Identification lost during sample preparation

*"During ICP-OES sample preparation, an analyst was digesting six cathode powder samples in sequence. Digestion vessels were labeled with sequential numbers written on masking tape, which was not cross-referenced to the original sample identification codes in the laboratory's records. Two vessels' labels became illegible after exposure to acid fumes, and the analyst could not confirm with certainty which digestion solution corresponded to which original sample."*

This is as serious as a nonconformity gets under this clause. Once identity is lost, every result associated with the affected items becomes unusable — there is no way to know which result belongs to which sample. Section 7.2's requirement for durable, appropriate labeling exists precisely because laboratory environments (acid fumes, solvents, refrigeration, humidity) are hostile to ordinary labels. Masking tape and pen is not adequate for an acid digestion workflow.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-minor">Minor</span> Storage location not tracked

*"The laboratory maintained a sample register showing receipt of items but did not track their current storage location. Locating a specific sample required physically searching through several storage areas. On the day of the audit, one item could not be located within the time available and was found after the audit concluded."*

Being unable to promptly locate an item does not necessarily mean it has been lost or mishandled, but it demonstrates a gap in the traceability system. Section 9.3 requires storage location to be tracked and kept current — not because losing track of an item once is catastrophic, but because a laboratory that cannot reliably say where its items are cannot reliably demonstrate that they have been protected.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-minor">Minor</span> Retention period not defined or applied

*"Samples of anode material from jobs completed 18 months earlier remained in general storage with no defined retention period and no record of a disposition decision having been made. The Technical Manager confirmed there was no policy specifying how long completed items should be retained before return or disposal."*

Without a defined retention period, items simply accumulate indefinitely, consuming storage capacity and, for materials with any hazard classification, creating an unmanaged and growing liability. Section 10.1 requires a retention period to be defined; Section 10.2 requires an active disposition decision at the end of it — retention should be a deliberate choice, not a default that happens by not making a decision.

### <span className="nc-tag nc-major">Major</span> Hazardous item disposed of without authorization or record

*"A quantity of expired electrolyte solvent was disposed of by pouring it down a laboratory drain. No disposal record existed, and the Safety Officer had not been consulted. When questioned, the analyst responsible stated they were not aware that a documented disposal process applied to small quantities."*

This finding combines an environmental and safety failure with a documentation failure. Flammable and chemically hazardous materials require a controlled disposal route regardless of quantity, and that route requires sign-off and a record under Section 10.4. The absence of awareness among laboratory staff points to a training gap that the laboratory should address alongside the immediate corrective action.

### How to use these examples

Item handling nonconformities cluster around a single theme: a gap between what happened to an item and what the laboratory can demonstrate happened to it. For any item currently in your laboratory, you should be able to answer, with a record to support each answer: what condition did it arrive in, how do we know it is still correctly identified, where is it now, and what will happen to it next. If any of those four questions cannot be answered from your records, you have found a gap before an auditor does.
